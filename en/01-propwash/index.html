Propwash

Don't fall into your own wind.

▶  Present the slides

Every drone flies by pushing air down. Four propellers spinning tens of thousands of times a minute throw a strong stream of air underneath the quad. That's what keeps it in the sky. But that air doesn't disappear. It turns into a column of fast, swirling, messy air below and behind the drone. Pilots call it propwash.

Most of the time you fly away from it, into clean air, and you never notice. The trouble starts when you fall back into it: dropping straight down, cutting the throttle at the bottom of a dive, or snapping out of a tight turn. Suddenly the propellers are trying to grip air that is already moving in every direction.

Thrust becomes uneven, the flight controller fights to keep the drone level, overcorrects, corrects again, and you see it as a shake or wobble in your video, usually right when you want things to be calm.

It's the same thing F1 drivers call "dirty air": follow another car too closely and your car loses grip, because it's driving through the turbulence of the car in front. Helicopters know an extreme version too: the vortex ring state, which is why pilots avoid descending straight down at low speed.

How to fly out of it

  • Don't come straight down. Descend at an angle with some forward speed, so you keep moving into clean air.
  • Don't cut the throttle completely in a dive. A little throttle keeps the props working.
  • Be smooth when exiting dives and turns. Roll out of the dirty air, don't punch straight back into it.

The drone matters too: straight, balanced props and a solid build suffer far less, and good tuning can calm propwash down a lot. But the first fix is always in your thumbs.

Don't fall into your own wind.
