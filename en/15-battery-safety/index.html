Battery safety

At the first sign: get it out, or get out. Fast.

▶  Present the slides

A LiPo battery is safe when you treat it right. Thousands of packs are charged and flown every day without a problem. But when a LiPo fails, it fails fast and hot. This guide is about seeing it coming, charging and storing packs so a failure stays small, and what to do when it happens. How a battery works is in the talk "How batteries work".

What makes a LiPo burn

A LiPo has no protection circuit inside, so almost every fire starts with one of these:

  • Crash damage. A hit can crush a cell inside the pack. It may look fine and fail later, even days later, on the charger.
  • Overcharging. The wrong chemistry or cell count set on the charger, or a broken charger.
  • Short circuit. Bare leads touching, a screw or a sharp edge through the pack, a cut wire.
  • Puffed packs. Gas inside means the cells are damaged, and it only gets worse.
  • Heat. A hot car, direct sun, or charging a pack that's still hot from flying.
  • Emptying it too far. Below 3.0 V per cell the cells get damaged, and a damaged cell is risky to charge.

Puffed or damaged? Retire it

A pack doesn't heal. Retire it when it's swollen or soft, dented or cut, gets hot after a gentle flight, or has a cell that drops much faster than the others. If in doubt, it's done.

Don't charge it, don't fly it, don't press it flat and don't puncture it. Put it in a LiPo bag or a metal box, away from anything that burns, and dispose of it (see below).

How a LiPo fire goes

A LiPo fire usually goes in four stages:

  1. Puff. The pack swells: gas is building up inside.
  2. Hiss. The pack vents gas and smoke. Flames often follow within about 10 seconds.
  3. Fire. Jets of flame. One cell heats the next, and the next one goes every 10 to 15 seconds. A whole pack burns in a minute or two.
  4. Sleeping dragon. It looks out, but the cells can still be hot inside and flare up again, even hours later.

After a crash there may be no warning at all: a crushed cell can go straight to fire.

Know the smell

The smell is often the first warning, before you see or hear anything. It's sickeningly sweet, like strong flowers or cheap air freshener. That smell has no place in a technical area. If you smell it, find the source now.

Don't breathe the smoke: it's toxic and can contain hydrogen fluoride. And don't wait for the smell. Some of the gas has no smell at all, so no smell doesn't mean all clear. Hissing, popping or crackling from a pack means the same thing.

Seconds, and a bigger flame than you think

When a pack goes, you have seconds, not minutes. The flame is a jet, not a candle: it shoots out sideways and up, much further than you expect. In our tests it reached 4 to 5 metres. The smoke can fill a room in moments.

That's why the message is simple: at the first sign, get the pack out if you safely can, or get yourself out. Fast. Never try to save the pack.

Where to charge

Charge every pack as if it's going to catch fire. Clear the whole area, not just the desk.

  • A fireproof surface: stone, tiles, metal, or at least a ceramic tile under the bag. Never a bed, a sofa, a carpet or a wooden desk.
  • Clear around it: nothing that burns within several metres. Curtains, paper, cardboard boxes, other packs.
  • A LiPo bag or a metal box. It holds the flames in for a while. It doesn't put the fire out.
  • Never unattended. Stay in the room. Don't charge overnight or while you're out.
  • A smoke detector in the room where you charge, and a clear way out.
  • A cool pack in a cool room. Let a pack cool down after flying. Never charge below 0 °C or in direct sun.

At the club we charge at the charging station, with fireproof surfaces, LiPo bags and water at hand. At home, find a spot that's as close to that as you can get: a stone or tiled floor, away from beds and curtains, ideally near a door.

If it catches fire

Kids: shout for an adult, get out and stay out.

Adults:

  1. Unplug the charger at the wall socket, not by grabbing the battery.
  2. If it's already burning indoors: get out, close the door and call 112.
  3. Use lots of water. A LiPo is a lithium-ion battery, and water is right for it: it cools the cells. Powder and CO₂ extinguishers don't cool it, so it flares up again.
  4. When it stops hissing and spitting, move it with a shovel or tongs into a metal bucket of water, outside. Sand doesn't cool it.
  5. Leave it in the water for a day. It can flare up hours later. Then take it to a jäätmejaam.

More in the talk "Safety".

Storing your packs

A stored pack can still burn, so store it so that a fire stays small. Bring it to storage voltage, 3.8 V per cell, if you won't fly in the next day or two. Keep packs in a fireproof box (a metal ammo box with the rubber seal removed, so pressure can escape) or a LiPo bag, not in a drawer or a backpack. Keep them cool and dry: room temperature or a bit cooler, never in a car or in the sun.

Getting rid of a pack

Never put a LiPo in the household rubbish. In a bin truck it gets crushed, and crushed LiPos start fires. Instead:

  1. Empty it. Discharge it slowly with the charger, as low as the charger lets you.
  2. Tape the leads so the wires can't touch.
  3. Take it back to a jäätmejaam, or to the battery box in a shop that sells batteries. It's free.

A puffed or hot pack: keep it outside in a metal box or a bucket of sand until you take it away.

Flying with LiPos

Going to a race abroad? Spare LiPos travel in your hand luggage only, never in checked bags.

Pack sizeRule
up to 100 WhAllowed in hand luggage, no approval needed
100 to 160 WhAirline approval needed, at most 2
over 160 WhNot allowed

A 6S 1300 mAh pack is 28.9 Wh, well under the limit. Put each pack in a LiPo bag or its own pouch with the leads taped, and fly with packs at storage voltage or lower. Some airlines ask for 25 % or less. Some airlines also limit how many spares you can carry, and the rules change, so check your airline before every trip.
